The market sets the return. Inflation picks the death year.

Retirement failure tables blame the market, but a fixed portfolio can hand you a fixed return and still go broke, and the one moving part that decides when is the raise you give yourself. Run it flat so the market drops out of the argument entirely: a million dollars earning 7 percent every year, forty thousand withdrawn at the start, checks raised with inflation and nothing else. At 2 percent inflation the plan survives sixty years and the balance stands past eleven million. At 6 percent the same portfolio, same return, same first check, dies in year 29 with the year's $204,467 check unpaid. Same market. The spread is entirely the raise.

The mechanics are dull and that is the point. A 4 percent check escalator runs the money up first: balance $1,474,937 with a $87,645 check at year 20, $1,379,352 and $129,736 at year 30, $1,074,412 and $157,844 at year 35. Then the checks catch the return: $460,354 at year 40, $287,095 at year 41, $93,489 at year 42, and year 43 fails with a $207,711 check outstanding. Three percent inflation never fails; its year 20 balance, the low point before the compounding takes over, is $1,661,661. A 5 percent world pulls the death to year 34, a 6 percent world to year 29, and none of it was the market, because the market never moved off 7.

So the inflation assumption is not a footnote under the returns column, it is the load-bearing number, and it deserves the harder audit. Two plans can quote the same portfolio, the same first-year check, the same history of returns and differ by a decade of life on the inflation line alone. Price your raises before you price your returns: the money lasts exactly as long as the raise does.
